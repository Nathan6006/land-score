# Methods

This page explains how Land Score works, for a science fair judge or a forester. Numbers live
in [`RESULTS.md`](RESULTS.md) and [`SENSITIVITY.md`](SENSITIVITY.md), which are generated by
the code. Every change of plan is logged in [`DECISIONS.md`](DECISIONS.md).

## The question

Roots of Tomorrow plants native trees on land that owners offer. For an offered plot, we want
to know how readily trees should establish there, before anyone plants.

The score describes **site potential**. It is not a promise that planted trees will survive.
Survival depends heavily on care after planting, especially weed control and deer protection.

## Why a stand-in is needed

No public dataset records where trees were planted in Ohio and whether they lived. So the
model learns from something that can be observed everywhere: **farm fields that stopped being
farmed, and how much tree cover grew back on its own.** Land where trees returned quickly
without help is land where trees establish easily.

This stand-in has a known gap: natural regrowth needs a seed source nearby, while planted
seedlings do not. See "Limits" below.

## Study area and grid

Nine counties in Northeast Ohio: Geauga (pilot), Cuyahoga, Lorain, Ashtabula, Lake, Portage,
Summit, Trumbull, and Medina. Everything is put on one grid of 30 m squares (pixels), the
same grid the land cover maps use (CONUS Albers, EPSG:5070).

## Data

All sources are public and need no account. `landscore fetch` downloads only the part
covering each county and records each file's source and checksum in `data/manifest.json`.

| Layer | Source | Years | Native detail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Land cover | USGS Annual NLCD, Collection 1.1 | 1985–2024, yearly | 30 m |
| Tree canopy cover | USFS Tree Canopy Cover, Science product v2025-6 | 1985–2025, yearly | 30 m |
| Soil | USDA gNATSGO map units + Soil Data Access tables | survey | 10 m map units |
| Elevation | USGS 3DEP 1/3 arc-second | — | about 10 m |
| Climate | NOAA nClimGrid normals | 1991–2020 average | about 5 km |
| County outlines | US Census cartographic boundaries 2023 | — | 1:500,000 |

## Labels: what the model is trained to predict

For every pixel we read its 40-year land cover history and apply these rules
(`config/study.yaml`, tested in `tests/test_labels.py`):

1. **Farmed.** The pixel was pasture/hay or cultivated crops for at least 3 years in a row.
2. **Stopped.** It then left those classes and stayed out for at least 3 years in a row. The
   first year out is the *abandonment year*, and it must fall in 1988–2009.
3. **Not a human conversion.** If the pixel became developed land or water, or was farmed
   again, before the outcome was measured, it is dropped. A person decided its fate, which
   says nothing about the site.
4. **Open at the start.** Tree canopy the year before abandonment was at most 10%.
5. **Outcome.** Tree canopy cover **15 years after** the abandonment year.

The outcome is used two ways: as a percent (regression) and as yes/no, "did canopy reach
40%?" (binary). The score uses the binary form.

Only abandoned fields are used. If all land were included, the model would mostly learn who
chose to stop farming, which is a human decision and tells little about the land.

**What we found:** farmland here is persistent. In Geauga, 77% of pixels farmed in 1990 were
still pasture in 2024, and most that changed became developed. Abandoned-and-left-alone land is
rare, so labels are a small share of each county, and many sit on field edges next to forest.

## Features: what the model may look at

Only things knowable before regrowth began (`src/landscore/features/registry.py`):

- **Soil:** drainage class, available water storage, depth to water table, pH, sand, clay,
  organic matter, hydrologic group, the survey's own seedling mortality rating, forest site index.
- **Terrain:** elevation, slope, direction the slope faces, wetness index, position on the slope.
- **Climate:** average yearly precipitation and temperature.
- **Landscape at the start:** distance to the nearest forest, percent forest within 100 m and
  500 m, and whether the field was pasture or crops. These come from the land cover map of
  the year *before* abandonment.

**Leakage rule.** A model can cheat if a feature secretly contains the answer, for example a
recent satellite image that shows the trees that grew. So every vegetation-related feature
carries its measurement year, and a test fails if that year is not earlier than the
abandonment year (`tests/test_leakage.py`). Soil, terrain, and climate are treated as fixed
properties of the site. Recent imagery is never a feature.

## Models

Simple baselines come first, because a complicated model is only worth using if it beats them:

1. The soil survey's seedling mortality rating alone.
2. Distance to the nearest forest alone.
3. Logistic regression on all features.

Main models: random forest and gradient-boosted trees (scikit-learn). The score is the
model's estimated probability, times 100, that a pixel reaches the canopy target.

No large language model is used anywhere in scoring.

## Validation

- **County holdout (headline).** Train on eight counties, test on the ninth, and repeat for
  each county. This asks whether the model works somewhere it has never seen.
- **Spatial blocks.** Hold out 5 km squares.
- **Random split.** Shown only for contrast. Neighboring pixels look alike, so a random split
  lets the model peek at the test area and gives numbers that are too high. The gap between
  random and county holdout shows how much.
- **Calibration.** When the model says 70%, do about 70% of such pixels reach the target?
- **Feature importance** with SHAP values, to see what drives the score.
- **Sensitivity.** Results are recomputed after dropping tiny patches and forest-edge pixels,
  and label counts are shown for other lags and thresholds.

Still to do, with reserved space in `RESULTS.md`:

- **Forester check.** A forester ranks about 20 plots without seeing scores.
- **Roots case studies.** Scores for Roots' own planting sites against what happened there.
- **Lidar check.** Compare canopy labels with canopy height from 2019–2020 lidar.

## The plot report

`landscore report plot.geojson` returns, for the pixels inside the plot:

1. **Score map and summary** from the trained model, using today's landscape (2024 land cover)
   for the forest-distance features.
2. **Species fit**, a lookup table matching native trees to the plot's drainage and pH. It is
   a draft compiled from general references and must be reviewed by a forester.
3. **Planning flags**, simple rules: wet soil, competing vegetation, likely deer pressure
   (a rough proxy from the forest/open-land mix), steep slope.
4. The site-potential caveat.

## Limits

- **Stand-in, not survival.** The model predicts natural regrowth on old fields. Planted
  trees with good care can do well on low-scoring sites, and badly on high-scoring ones
  without care.
- **Seed source.** Distance to forest matters a lot for natural regrowth but much less for
  planted seedlings. A high score partly means "close to forest", which a planting crew does
  not need.
- **Edge noise.** Many labeled pixels are single pixels along field edges, where a class
  change can be a mapping artifact. See `SENSITIVITY.md`.
- **Map errors.** Land cover and canopy maps come from satellite models and carry their own
  errors. Canopy percent is noisy for individual pixels.
- **Coarse layers.** Soil map units are generalized. Climate is on a 5 km grid.
- **Small plots.** A one-acre plot is about 4 pixels. Read the score as a rough guide.
- **Deer and management are not modeled.** Deer pressure is only a rough flag.
- **One region.** The model has only seen Northeast Ohio.
